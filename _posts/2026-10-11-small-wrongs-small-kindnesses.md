Today, on my way downstairs, I happened to run into a courier who had just come up in the elevator to my floor. Later I learned he had come to deliver a package to my home. He stepped out of the elevator and I stepped in. I did not wait for him. I simply closed the doors and left.

I did not do well.

I am thirty-five. I may not have seen much great good or great evil, but I have dealt with all sorts of people. Lately I keep thinking of the famous words of the Former Lord, Liu Bei: do not do evil because it is small; do not leave good undone because it is small.

At my age, why am I still thinking about sayings from a children's primer of Chinese classics?

I don't know myself. Perhaps all these years have given me a new understanding of society, and new reflection on myself.

I don't know how virtuous I am, how upright, how selfish. In many situations it seemed that I could do it this way, or I could do it that way. On many small details, I relaxed what I asked of myself.

For example, during the summer vacation a few months ago, my son lost a basket of game tokens at the arcade. I searched without finding it, and when I saw an unattended basket of tokens, I took it as the one my son had lost and kept it. In the end I found out it actually belonged to another child who had lost it. Although I made it up to that child, I feel ashamed of what I did. I could have simply, freely taken it to the front desk.

The Huainanzi also says: feathers piled up will sink a boat; many light loads will break an axle. If I claim to be an upright person in big matters, then I must hold myself to doing well in small matters too. As for how exactly to do well, I believe the Former Lord's words can serve as a guide: do not do evil because it is small; do not leave good undone because it is small.

At thirty-five, I know the world is not one where good is rewarded with good and evil with evil. The world only rewards the strong. I may have seen many successful people whose conduct in matters great and small was not necessarily good. In particular, a considerable share of the successful may well have built their success on wrongdoing. But that is other people's business. Refraining from small wrongs is to give myself peace of mind; doing small kindnesses is not done in expectation of a reward. I just want to be someone with real love in my heart. Like Wong Ka Kui.

No matter how great a person's achievements, how much power they hold, how much money they can make, or how great the works they can create, an accident may take their life. But as the words on Wong Ka Kui's tombstone say, life is not about what you gain, only about what you have done.

I want to do right by myself and live as the self I hold as an ideal. A self with real love in my heart.
