<p class="intro">Synopsis: After time travel was invented, the law, in order to preserve world order, required travelers to obey the laws of the time and place they visit. Otherwise, once they returned to the future, they would be tried under the laws of that time.</p>

## Chapter One

With a faint hum, the pale blue time gate opened. It looked just like the ones in the science fiction films I had watched from several hundred years ago. I never knew whether the ancients had an astonishingly accurate imagination, or whether later scientists deliberately designed it to look the way our ancestors had imagined.

"What are you staring at? Let's go." Captain Chen glanced back at me.

I broke into a run and followed the others. Ten meters, five meters, one meter. Right in front of me. I shut my eyes and pushed off hard. The sound around me vanished. Before I could react, I stepped on something and fell flat on the ground.

The ground was soft and wet. A jumble of noise suddenly burst through my eardrums and flooded my brain.

"You all right?"

I opened my eyes, and a hand hauled me up. Then came Captain Chen's rain-soaked face and the rolling dark clouds overhead. Rain pelted my face, and I wiped off the mud without thinking.

The Time Police station was gone. The few of us were standing in Scotland, in the year 1623.

"Come on. Get ready to make the arrest."

I made one of these expensive trips through time almost every week. The reason for each arrest was different. Unlike in old science fiction novels, people who actually travel through time have almost no ability to change history. Once they go back, they are no different from anyone else. In fact, because they rely so heavily on modern technology, they are often worse off than the ancients, both physically and mentally. So the idea of using time travel to do something earth-shattering is pure fantasy. Of course, a trip has to span at least fifty years. I never quite understood the logic behind that rule, but according to Captain Chen, it was to stop people from going back a short way to buy lottery tickets or stocks.

"Anyway, keeping order in time is up to people like us." When I first joined the squad, Captain Chen told me, "Move fast. Charge in and grab the target, and ignore whatever else you see. Once you have them, pull out right away."

"Even in front of a crowd?"

"Even then."

"Won't that affect history?"

"No. They'll just think they've seen a ghost," Captain Chen said. "As if the ancients didn't see enough ghosts already."

Tommy stayed behind to guard the time gate. The rest of us set out on the mission, with Anna leading the way. She had volunteered, because this place happened to be her hometown. Her hometown more than eleven hundred years earlier, of course.

Through the pouring rain, we crossed a wood and reached a muddy village. In the dark, lit by flashes of lightning, we could make out a few lopsided thatched huts. I noticed what seemed to be the vague outline of a castle in the distance, but that was clearly not our target.

Anna led us to a thatched hut that looked ready to collapse in the storm.

"This is it." She glanced at the holographic map in her hand.

Given the state of the door, I doubted it was necessary, but Captain Chen still stepped forward, exchanged a look with the rest of us, and kicked the door open.

I charged in with the others. In the dim firelight, several children lying on the floor sat up and stared at us in terror. Captain Chen and Mike were dragging a woman who was struggling hard to break free. She wore a filthy robe and was crying out something. Then Anna said:

"Wrong person, it's not her!"

At that moment, a young girl came out of the inner room, holding a steaming earthenware pot.

"Let her go. I'll come with you!"

Captain Chen and Mike exchanged an awkward glance and let go of the woman. Seeing that the girl made no move to run, Captain Chen said:

"Hurry up!"

"Give me one minute."

The girl handed the pot to her companion, who was still crying, and said something to her in a low voice. I could not understand a word of it.

The woman held her and wept. The children gathered around too. The girl kissed each of them. Some of the children tried to hold her back, but Anna lifted them away.

Target secured. We packed up and went home.

## Chapter Two

"All rise."

The judge took the bench. Once he was seated, the clerk signaled everyone to sit.

"June 13, 2799. The trial of Criminal Case No. 1498 of 2799 is now in session. Bring in the defendant, Li Chao."

"Will the prosecution and the defense state their appearances."

"Yang Yu, First-Grade Prosecutor, Temporal Prosecutor's Office."

"Defense counsel Richard Deng, Richard Law Firm."

"The prosecution may begin its statement."

"Thank you, Your Honor. The defendant, Li Chao, male, 44 years old, applied for time travel in January 2798. The application was approved in March 2798. He carried out the trip in June 2798. His destination was Zhejiang, China, in 1233 BC, with a planned stay of three months. During the trip, the defendant smuggled in a modern knife, killed six local people, and overstayed. In February 2799, the Time Police issued a wanted notice. In March of the same year, Criminal Team 1 of the Third Squadron of the Time Police arrested the defendant and brought him to justice. The prosecution now charges him with intentional homicide under the current Criminal Code."

"The defense may begin its questioning."

"Thank you, Your Honor. Mr. Li, what is your occupation?"

"I'm the chairman of Elite Games."

"Why did you make this trip through time?"

"To gather material for a new game we were developing, *Ancient Slaughter*."

"Please describe what happened in this case."

"Objection. The question is overly broad."

"Sustained. Deng, please narrow the question."

"Yes, Your Honor. Mr. Li, why did you come into conflict with the six deceased?"

"No reason. I just wanted to see how they reacted as they died. It was an experiment, to give our upcoming game *Ancient Slaughter* more realistic material."

"Did you know who the people were that you used in your experiment?"

"Farmers, I guess? Fishermen? Herders? I'm not really sure."

"In any case, they were not nobles, correct?"

"Right, definitely not nobles. Their houses, their clothes, all very shabby. You could tell from their hands and faces they were the bottom of society, rough as anything. Not nobles."

"So you deliberately chose commoners for your experiment, correct?"

"Objection. Defense counsel is deliberately substituting the word 'experiment' for 'killing.'"

"Overruled. Mr. Li, you may answer Deng's question."

"Yes, I deliberately chose commoners."

"Deng, if you have more questions, you may continue."

"What did you use for the experiment?"

"A knife."

"What kind of knife? What brand, what model?"

"I don't remember the brand or model. Just an ordinary knife."

"Where did you get the knife?"

"At the Jiajiale supermarket downstairs from my place."

"Do you remember the price of the knife?"

"Twenty-nine ninety. The cheapest one."

"So you spent twenty-nine ninety to buy..."

"No, I'm a premium member at that supermarket, so in the end I got twenty percent off..."

A few stifled laughs came from the gallery.

The judge looked up, and the courtroom fell quiet again.

"Your Honor, I have no further questions."

"Prosecutor Yang, you may cross-examine."

Yang Yu stood and walked up to the defendant.

"Mr. Li, the knife you used was made in the modern era, correct?"

"Yes."

"Produced with modern industrial technology?"

"I suppose so. I don't know how it was made."

"You knew it was not made in 1233 BC?"

"Of course."

"You carried this knife through the time gate?"

"Yes."

"That knife is made of modern steel?"

"I don't know."

"Is it sharper and sturdier than the knives the local people had in the thirteenth century BC?"

"Maybe."

"Were the six people you killed carrying weapons at the time?"

"No."

"Did they attack you?"

"No."

"So you used a modern knife to kill six unarmed local commoners who did not attack you?"

"Yes."

"The prosecution has no further questions."

Yang Yu returned to his seat.

"Deng, do you have any more questions?"

"Just one, Your Honor."

Richard Deng walked back to Li Chao.

"Mr. Li, setting aside for the moment the era in which the knife was made, was there any difference between the way you used it to kill and the way the local people used metal blades to kill?"

"No. You just hack with the knife."

"Did you use any modern fighting techniques?"

"I don't know any fighting techniques. It was just like this." Li Chao raised his handcuffed hands and made a chopping motion.

"No further questions."

Li Chao returned to his seat at the defense table.

"The defense may now present its argument."

"Thank you, Your Honor. The defense does not dispute the facts as found in this case. However, the defense submits that this prosecution should not proceed under the current Criminal Code. Under Article 3 of the Temporal Liability Act, when a time traveler, in the course of time travel, commits against the residents of that time and place, by means consistent with that time and place, an offense defined by the law of that time and place, the traveler shall be tried under the law of that time and place. The defendant Li Chao traveled to the Zhejiang region of China in 1233 BC and killed commoners with a knife. This method did not exceed comparable methods of that time and place. According to the reconstruction of local tribal customary law by the Institute of Temporal Historical Appraisal, anyone who killed a commoner of his own tribe was fined three head of cattle. After returning to the present, the defendant has already paid a monetary fine equal in value to eighteen head of cattle. He should not be prosecuted again under the current Criminal Code."

"Objection. The court should note that the defendant committed the crime with a modern knife. This does not fall within the scope of committing an offense 'by means consistent with that time and place' under Article 3 of the Temporal Liability Act. The workmanship and strength of a modern knife clearly exceed the level of local craftsmanship in the thirteenth century BC. Under the judgment in Criminal Case No. 265 of 2788, committing an offense with a tool beyond the craftsmanship and technology of that time and place does not constitute means consistent with that time and place."

"Your Honor, on this point, the defense's expert witness has already testified. Although Li Chao used a modern knife, comparable metal chopping weapons were already commonly held and used by the aristocracy of that time and place. A modern knife may be superior in the strength of its metal, but because the ordinary modern knife Li Chao carried was merely a household item, its killing efficiency did not exceed that of the weapons used by the local aristocracy. Therefore, the defendant Li Chao's conduct fully meets the definition of committing an offense 'by means consistent with that time and place' under Article 3 of the Temporal Liability Act."

## Chapter Three

"Some people worry that if a time criminal kills someone who happens to be the ancestor of a person living today, it would directly change history, or even make that modern person disappear.

"In fact, the odds of that are vanishingly small. Keep in mind that every one of us in this room, going back twenty generations, has over a million so-called direct ancestors.

"Many people assume that every ancestor leaves a little bit of DNA in us, growing ever fainter. That is not the case. Even if an ancient person really is the ancestor of some modern person, after dozens of generations of inheritance, it is quite likely that not a single segment of that ancestor's autosomal DNA remains in the modern person.

"So the probability that killing a random ancient person happens to change some modern person's genes is very low, and naturally it would have no direct effect on that person...

"That's all for today. Thank you, everyone."

The lights came on. A smattering of polite applause filled the room. Captain Chen woke up, rubbed his eyes, and clapped along with everyone else. The professor, invited from some university or other, left. I closed my notebook and got ready to head back to the office. This was the station's monthly training session on the theory of time travel enforcement. Many people thought today's course, *The Ethics of Time Travel*, was a complete waste of time, but I figured taking some notes couldn't hurt. Captain Chen teased me that I was angling to get into the Prosecutor's Office, and begged me, if I ever got promoted, to go easy on the cases he sent over.

Tommy came over, hunched forward.

"Boss, you really should take a look at the file on the Lin Xinyan case."

"Which one?"

"1623, Scotland, the young girl."

"Oh, isn't that just a simple case of illegal overstay, failing to return on time? What's there to look at?"

"The criminal prosecution section doesn't see it that way."

I glanced at Tommy. His face was dead serious.

"Anna is furious right now..."

"All right. Let's go to your office."

Tommy's office was something of an oddity at the station: neat and spotless.

He spread the materials from the Lin Xinyan case across the desk. Every document had sticky notes of different colors on it.

"We did report it as an illegal overstay, but the prosecution section found..." Tommy pulled out one document and handed it to me. "They found this."

I flipped through it. It was a set of photos from our enforcement recording at the scene.

"What's the problem with this?"

"That pot."

"Hm?"

"There were herbs in the pot."

"Right. Miss Lin stuck her nose where it didn't belong. A fresh medical school graduate, she took pity on people during her trip and brewed some herbs to treat the local children," I said. "Those factors should reduce her fine and her time in custody."

"After 1563, Scottish law made witchcraft a capital crime. A young woman of unknown origin, using knowledge the locals couldn't understand, brews herbs and cures several sick children. The prosecution section believes her conduct meets the definition of witchcraft at that time and place."

I looked up sharply. Tommy was staring straight at me, his eyes bright to the point of being frightening.

"What's the recommended sentence?"

"Burning at the stake."

"Burned to death?"

"Yes. Burned alive."

"That's absurd..."

## Chapter Four

"What? Acquitted?"

When Captain Chen brought the news back to the station, the office erupted. Tommy turned on the TV, where the news was covering the case. Li Chao and his lawyer were surrounded by a crush of reporters. Farther off there were protesters, but they were held well back by a police cordon.

"Society's judgment of the same conduct changes over time. Plenty of behavior the ancients considered normal would be an outright crime today. Conversely, plenty of things we take for granted today might have gotten you drowned in a pig cage in ancient times. The reason the United Nations enacted the Temporal Liability Act..."

"How is that possible? Li Chao killed six people for no reason!"

"Right. We risked our necks to bring that animal back..."

"What can you do? Thousands of years ago, killing a commoner cost three cattle at most. If you killed someone from another tribe, you'd even get a cow as a reward."

"But the knife he used..."

"The judge found the knife was no deadlier than the knives of the local nobility back then."

"Why would anyone make such a rotten law?" Anna, in a fit of rage, tore the papers in her hands to shreds.

In the commotion, Tommy glanced at me. I nodded and walked over to Captain Chen.

"Chen, a word."

The three of us went into Captain Chen's office. Tommy shut the door.

"If the prosecution section goes ahead with the Lin Xinyan case like this, something's going to go wrong."

"What's wrong with that case?"

"A girl just out of medical school saves up for two or three years for a graduation trip through time. She travels to Scotland, sees a few sick children, can't bear it, and brews them some herbs. And for that she gets convicted of witchcraft? Sentenced to burn? Are we supposed to just stand by and watch a young girl burned alive? That's..."

I raised a hand and cut off Tommy, who was getting more and more worked up.

Captain Chen looked at us coldly and said nothing.

"Chen," I said, "we're the ones who brought her back. We owe her family an explanation."

"An explanation? What explanation? We are law enforcement. Our mission is to enforce the law, firmly."

"But sooner or later this is going to go wrong..." Tommy protested.

"No buts," Captain Chen said. "As if we haven't seen the law override sympathy plenty of times before. Liu, you've been at this station a long time. Tommy is new and doesn't know any better. Are you going to act like you don't either?"

Captain Chen pulled the door open and left. Tommy and I were left in the office. We were silent for a while, as if waiting for Captain Chen's words to fully clear from the air before we spoke. Then we heard Captain Chen bellow outside:

"What are you all staring at? What's there to look at in a lawyer who can't tell right from wrong and turns black into white? If you don't like it, go find him at the Richard firm!"

"Boss, what do we do?" Tommy was clutching the file so tightly that his hands trembled slightly. "Is there really nothing we can do?"

I looked back at the TV screen outside.

"If we can't do anything, we'll find someone who can."

## Chapter Five

Richard Law Firm. Richard Deng sat behind a desk as wide as a ping-pong table, leafing through the materials we had handed him. His spacious, bright office occupied a corner of the building's twenty-third floor. Outside the extravagant two-hundred-seventy-degree floor-to-ceiling windows, traffic flowed endlessly, and the most prosperous heart of the city lay in full view.

"I'm afraid this won't work," Richard Deng said. "I have a lot of sympathy for this girl. But a young woman of unknown origin brews herbs and cures several sick children. In seventeenth-century Scotland, it's no surprise that she would be judged a witch. Historically, during this period, the women burned at the stake in Scotland numbered in the hundreds, if not thousands."

"But what she used was modern medical knowledge. Using modern medical knowledge to treat illness and save lives, doesn't that go beyond the comparable means of that time and place?"

"What did she brew the herbs in? An earthenware pot. How did she brew them? Over a raw wood fire. And the herbs? Picked nearby. Everything she used was a means of that time and place."

"But her prescription was obviously based on modern medical knowledge."

"That is irrelevant to the case." Richard Deng looked utterly indifferent and glanced again at the gleaming watch on his wrist.

"How is that irrelevant?!" Tommy stood up, looking as if he were about to hit someone.

"Officer Tom! Please calm down." Richard Deng leaned hard on Tommy's name. "Look at it from the villagers' point of view at the time. Would you think this girl used some means beyond her era? No. What the locals saw and heard was a young girl brewing some herbs and curing a few children. If she were handed over to the governing authorities of that time and place for trial, she would certainly be convicted of witchcraft and sentenced to burn. That is strong proof that the means she used were consistent with the conditions of that time and place."

"Is there really nothing at all we can do?" Tommy asked. "Mr. Deng, you're a big-time lawyer. You must have a way, right? We'll pay. If the family doesn't have enough money, I'll organize a fundraiser!"

"Money is important, of course." Richard Deng picked up his teacup and took a sip. "But I'm afraid this time it isn't a problem money can solve."

He picked up the enforcement recording photos and looked at the fire under the pot.

"She gathered the herbs, lit the fire, brewed the medicine, and fed it to them, and everything she used throughout was of that time and place. If she had used a modern object at any single step, even just a lighter to light the firewood, I would be confident I could get the judge to find that she used means beyond that time and place."

Tommy froze for a moment.

Richard Deng kept his eyes on the materials and did not look at him.

"But a lawyer cannot create facts. If it isn't there, it isn't there."

When he finished, he glanced at his watch again.

"I'm sorry, I have another meeting starting shortly. Please excuse me. You're welcome to stay a while and finish your tea. It's top-grade pu'er from Yunnan, a gift from my client Li Chao in that last case. You can't get it on the market. And please remember to pay the consultation fee on your way out."

## Chapter Six

On the morning of Lin Xinyan's witchcraft trial, Anna was the first to arrive at the station, and she found the time gate still open. She checked the central control screen. The gate had been opened at 1 a.m. the night before. Only one name was on the travel list: Tom. The destination was Scotland, 1623.

Anna called Captain Chen and me at once. We rushed over. Tommy had not come back yet. But the control screen showed that his vital signs were still normal. Just as Captain Chen decided to take us through to find him, the time gate hummed. Tommy was back. He was covered in dirt and he was hurt.

Anna hurried over to help him up.

"What happened to you? Do you need a hospital?"

Tommy steadied himself with his hands on his knees, gently pushed Anna away, took something out of his pocket, and held it out to Captain Chen and me. His mud-covered palm opened. It was a lighter.

"Finally found it," Tommy panted. "When Lin Xinyan traveled, she smuggled in a lighter."

"The trial hasn't started yet. If we get the evidence in now and add a charge of smuggling a dangerous modern article, that's at least ten days to two weeks of detention for her. Plus a fine. What was the fine again? That case last time where someone brought cigarettes?"

"Fifty thousand," I said.

"Right, fifty thousand!" Tommy managed a smile, took the enforcement recorder off his shoulder, and handed it over as well. "How about that? Enough for a bonus for the squad?"

Captain Chen stared at Tommy in shock, then looked at the lighter and the recorder in his hands. Finally he took a deep breath, pulled out his phone, and walked out.

"The evidence was logged into storage just last night. Yes, an item missed at the arrest scene..."

I turned around and met Tommy's eyes. Anna was scolding him:

"Do you have a death wish? Do you think you're an engineer? Do you know how dangerous an unauthorized trip is? You could easily lose an arm or a leg! And if the time gate had been exposed and destroyed by the ancients, you'd never have made it back!"

"The villagers did this?" I asked.

"Yeah. They chased me with hoes. Said last time we kidnapped the woman who saved their lives."

"You've got guts," I said quietly. "Next time, file a report in advance. Now go get yourself checked at the hospital. Take the day off."

"Yes, thanks, Boss."

Anna looked baffled.

"What are you two smiling about?"

## Chapter Seven

The trial in the Lin Xinyan case lasted only forty-seven minutes.

The court found that, in the continuous act of lighting the fire and brewing the medicine, Lin Xinyan used a modern tool beyond the technological level of Scotland in 1623. Her conduct therefore did not meet the "means of that time and place" under Article 3 of the Temporal Liability Act, and she could not be held liable under the witchcraft laws of that time and place.

The witchcraft charge was dismissed.

For smuggling a dangerous modern article, overstaying, and intervening with local residents without approval, Lin Xinyan was sentenced to fifteen days of detention and a fine of fifty thousand.

Tommy did not go to court. He got eleven stitches at the hospital, and afterwards he faced an internal investigation for starting the time gate without approval and making an unauthorized high-risk trip.

Captain Chen wrote the report for him.

The report stated that while organizing the evidence from the arrest scene, Officer Tom discovered gaps in the enforcement record. Because the trial was imminent, and to prevent the loss of key evidence, he returned to the scene on his own to complete a supplemental investigation, as the situation was urgent and there was no time to seek approval through the chain of command.

The investigative committee ultimately suspended Tommy for three months and demoted him one grade.

Three months later, Tommy came back to the station. He was the same as before, first to arrive every day and last to leave. But not long after, he resigned.

I was held up for a while by some cases, and then I resigned too.

On my last day before leaving, Captain Chen put the Lin Xinyan case file on my desk.

"The case is closed."

"Mm."

"File it away."

He said that and left.

I opened the file and put the judgment, the witness statements, the enforcement records and the evidence log in order.

At the very bottom of the file was a photo of the lighter.

The lighter in the photo was caked in mud, its original color impossible to make out, but the scratch on its side was still clear. I had made it back when I used the lighter to open beer bottles.

The evidence log read:

Place found: Scotland, 1623, arrest scene.

Owner: Lin Xinyan.

Use: lighting firewood to brew medicine.

I signed my name in the box for the filing officer, then closed the file.

A few years later, I ran into Tommy by chance at a restaurant in Morocco.

He was tanned and much thinner, eating with a few people I didn't know.

We greeted each other like old friends meeting after a long time apart, and asked how each other had been. Neither of us mentioned the Time Police, or Lin Xinyan, or the lighter.

Just before I left, Tommy suddenly asked me:

"Boss, how come you don't smoke anymore?"

"I quit."

"When did you quit?"

I thought for a moment.

"After I lost my lighter."

Tommy lowered his head and gave a short laugh.

"Sorry."

"Nonsense. You'd love it if I smoked myself into lung cancer, wouldn't you?"

"Goodbye, then."

"Goodbye."

He left the restaurant with his companions.

After that, we never saw each other again.
